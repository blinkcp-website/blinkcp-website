**Format:** Carousel, 5 static slides (navy hook → 3 math slides over the fix & flip case-study photo → navy CTA)

5. CTA slide: "Run your numbers before you bid. blinkcp.com | 631.353.7022"

**Hook line:** "90% of purchase. 100% of rehab. There's still a ceiling on top of both — here's where it actually sits."

**Caption:**
"90% of purchase plus 100% of rehab" is the number investors hear first, but it's not the whole formula. There's a second test running in the background, and it's the one that actually caps your loan on a lot of deals: total loan amount can't exceed 75% of ARV. Whichever number — the purchase/rehab math or the ARV math — comes out lower is what you get financed.

Here's what that looked like on a real deal: a Columbus, OH flip at $185,000 purchase with a $62,000 rehab budget. Financed at 90% of purchase plus 100% of rehab, the loan came to $208,650. Projected ARV was $340,000, and 75% of that is $255,000 — comfortably above the $208,650 loan amount, so the purchase-and-rehab math was the number that governed here.

Flip the inputs and the ARV test can become the binding one fast — a thin rehab budget on a property with a modest ARV can get capped by the 75% line well before you hit 90%/100%. Know both numbers before you write an offer, not after your term sheet comes back lower than you expected.

## Post 3 — Friday — Market / Thought-Leadership Hot Take
**Theme:** Your homeowners policy doesn't cover the house you're about to gut
**Format:** Single static image (navy/gold branded graphic, no photo)

**Hook line:** "Your homeowners policy doesn't cover the house you're about to gut — and your lender knows it before you do."

**Caption:**
Hot take: the flips that stall mid-renovation over an insurance issue aren't unlucky — they're running the wrong policy.

A standard homeowners or landlord policy is underwritten around an occupied, stable property. Most carry a vacancy clause that suspends coverage once a home sits empty past a set window, commonly 30 to 60 days. A property mid-gut-renovation — vacant, open walls, contractors in and out — is exactly the risk profile those policies are written to exclude. That's what builder's risk insurance is for: a separate policy built around vacancy and active construction as the baseline, not the exception.

It's also why your lender won't release that first draw without proof it's active. On a fix & flip or construction loan, the property is the collateral, and the lender typically wants to be named loss payee or mortgagee, with coverage at least equal to the loan amount, confirmed before funding and at each inspection. A lapsed policy is one of the fastest ways to stall a draw.

One gap worth knowing: builder's risk covers the structure and materials, not general liability — a contractor's injured employee or damage to a neighbor's property during demo isn't what this policy is for. That's a separate conversation with your insurance agent, not an assumption to make on day one of demo.

Confirm coverage is active before closing, not after the first swing of a sledgehammer.

## Post 4 — Sunday — Myth-Busting / Engagement
**Theme:** DSCR loans don't cap how many rentals you can finance
**Format:** Carousel, 4 static slides (navy myth → 2 supporting slides → navy CTA)

**Slide breakdown:**
1. Navy myth slide: "Myth: there's a hard limit on how many rental properties you can finance."
2. "Truth: that cap is a conventional-mortgage rule, tied to your personal debt-to-income. DSCR loans qualify each property on its own rental income — so that ceiling generally doesn't apply."
3. "What actually slows scaling: a lender's total exposure limit to one borrower, reserve requirements that step up as your portfolio grows, and your own cash position for reserves — not a stated property count."
4. Navy CTA: "Scaling past a handful of rentals? Let's map the real constraints on your portfolio. blinkcp.com | 631.353.7022"

**Hook line:** "Myth: there's a hard limit on how many rental properties you can finance. There isn't — not the one you're thinking of."

**Caption:**
We hear this from investors scaling past their third or fourth rental: "I thought lenders only let you finance a handful of properties." That's a conventional-mortgage rule — Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines cap most borrowers because those loans qualify against your personal income and debt-to-income ratio. DSCR loans qualify each property on its own rental income instead, which is exactly why that property-count ceiling generally doesn't carry over.

That doesn't mean scaling is limitless. The real constraints are softer but just as real: many lenders cap total dollar exposure to a single borrower rather than counting properties, reserve requirements typically step up the more properties you hold, and geographic or property-type concentration can draw more scrutiny from a lender's risk appetite. Most investors hit their own cash-reserve ceiling before they ever hit a lender's.

The practical move as you grow: build relationships with more than one DSCR lender, keep reserves ahead of the minimum rather than right at it, and keep clean portfolio-level documentation ready. A blanket loan consolidating several properties under one note is also worth a conversation once you're past a handful.
