## Post 1 — Monday — Educational Carousel
**Theme:** The two reserve lines that quietly protect your deal
**Format:** Carousel, 6 slides

**Slide breakdown:**
1. Hook slide — navy/gold: "Two reserves. Two completely different jobs. Know which one your deal needs."
2. "🛠️ Interest Reserve (Fix & Flip) — a slice of your loan proceeds held back at closing to make your own monthly interest payments while the property isn't producing income."
3. "The math: Interest Reserve ≈ Loan Amount × Rate ÷ 12 × Months Held. On $260K at 10.5% over a 9-month hold, that's roughly $20,475 held back and drawn down automatically each month."
4. "🏗️ Contingency Reserve (Ground-Up Construction) — a slice of your construction budget set aside for costs outside your original line-item budget: site surprises, material price moves, change orders."
5. "Typical sizing: 5–10% of hard construction costs on a straightforward build; 10–15%+ when there's more unknown — an unfamiliar lot, a first-time builder, custom specs."
6. CTA slide: "Know which reserve applies before you lock your budget. blinkcp.com | 631.353.7022"

**Hook line:** "Your loan might be paying your own mortgage payment right now — here's how that works."

**Caption:**
Two reserve lines show up on different programs, and they do completely different jobs. Mixing them up is an easy way to misjudge how much cash you actually need at closing.

🛠️ On a fix & flip, an interest reserve is a portion of your loan held back to make your monthly interest payments automatically while the property isn't producing income — no rent, no sale, nothing coming in yet, but the payment is still due. Rough math: Loan Amount × Rate ÷ 12 × Months Held. On $260,000 at 10.5% over a 9-month hold, that's about $20,475 financed into the loan and drawn down for you each month.

🏗️ On a ground-up build, a contingency reserve is a slice of your construction budget set aside for the stuff that isn't in your line-item estimate — unexpected site conditions, a material price jump, a change order once framing's up. Lenders typically want to see 5–10% of hard costs reserved on a straightforward build, more like 10–15%+ when there's real unknown in the project.

Neither one is a red flag on your term sheet — they're built in on purpose. Know which applies to your deal before you plan your cash-to-close. blinkcp.com or DM us your scope.

**Hook line:** "A DSCR of 1.31 isn't just a number on a term sheet — it's about $627 a month in your pocket."

**Caption:**
DSCR gets thrown around as a qualifying ratio, but it's worth translating into actual dollars — because that's what it means for your cash flow every month.

The deal: a tenant-occupied Tampa, FL rental with a signed lease at $2,650/month. Purchase price $310,000, financed at 75% LTV ($232,500), closed as a 30-year fixed — no tax returns or employment verification, qualified entirely on the lease.

DSCR came in at 1.31. Run the ratio backward and that means roughly $2,023/month in total debt service (principal, interest, taxes, insurance) — which leaves about $627/month of rent above the payment. That gap is what absorbs a vacancy month, a repair, or just adds to your return.

A DSCR right at 1.0 means the rent barely clears the payment. A DSCR above 1.25 — like this one — means there's real room. Know which one you're buying before you close. blinkcp.com or DM us your rent roll and we'll run it.

## Post 3 — Friday — Market / Thought-Leadership Hot Take
**Theme:** Your ARV is an estimate until the appraiser says otherwise
**Format:** Single static image (navy/gold branded graphic, no photo)

**Hook line:** "You underwrote the deal at $380K ARV. The appraisal came back at $350K. That $30K gap just became your problem."

**Caption:**
Hot take: most "bad appraisal" stories aren't bad luck — they're an optimistic ARV that never got stress-tested before the offer went in.

Your ARV at underwriting is an estimate — built from comps, adjusted for your renovation scope, usually pulled by you, your agent, or a lender's review. The appraisal is the number that actually sets your loan amount, ordered from a licensed, independent appraiser. Any gap between the two is on you to plan for, not react to.

And it's not just a paper loss. Most fix & flip loans size against the lower of total project cost or a percentage of ARV — so a lower appraised value can shrink your maximum loan amount under the ARV test, meaning more cash out of your pocket at closing even if your rehab budget hasn't moved an inch.

The fix is boring but it works: pull comps conservatively (closed sales, not active listings), don't anchor to the single best comp in the neighborhood, and leave a financing cushion instead of committing every available dollar to the number you hope the appraiser agrees with. The investors who get surprised are usually the ones who built their offer around the best-case number instead of the representative one.

## Post 4 — Sunday — Myth-Busting / Engagement
**Theme:** Prepayment penalties on DSCR loans aren't a red flag
**Format:** Carousel, 4 static slides (navy myth → 2 supporting slides → navy CTA)

**Slide breakdown:**
1. Navy myth slide: "Myth: a prepayment penalty means something's wrong with the loan."
2. "Truth: it's standard on most DSCR rental loans — and it's usually what buys you the lower rate in the first place."
3. "The common structures: 5-4-3-2-1 step-down (5% Year 1, down to 1% Year 5, nothing after), or a shorter 3-2-1. Pick the structure that matches your real hold period, not the one that sounds best today."
4. Navy CTA: "Planning a BRRRR refi or a shorter hold? Let's match the structure to your exit. blinkcp.com | 631.353.7022"

**Hook line:** "Seeing a prepayment penalty on your DSCR term sheet doesn't mean you got a bad deal."

**Caption:**
We hear this one a lot: "why does my DSCR loan have a prepayment penalty — is that a bad sign?" No. It's standard, and it's usually the tradeoff that gets you the better rate in the first place.

DSCR loans are typically sold into securitizations, and the pricing assumes the loan stays in place for a while. The most common structure is a step-down — 5-4-3-2-1 means a 5% penalty on the unpaid balance if you pay off in Year 1, dropping a point a year until it hits zero in Year 6. A 3-2-1 structure does the same thing over three years. Shorter structures usually come with a slightly higher rate, since the lender gives up that protection sooner.

The real mistake isn't having a prepayment penalty — it's picking the wrong one for your plan. A buy-and-hold rental you're keeping for a decade should almost always take the cheapest structure available, since you're unlikely to ever trigger it. Running a BRRRR and planning to cash-out refinance once the property seasons? Model the penalty into your refi math before you choose — a shorter structure can pay for itself.
