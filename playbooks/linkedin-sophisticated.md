# LinkedIn — posts for experienced flippers, developers and builders

Written for people who already do this for a living. The test for every post:
would someone who has built ten houses learn something, or roll their eyes?

Everything here is derived from `case-studies.html`. The ratios are computed
from those figures, not asserted — and they reconcile exactly with the 90% of
total project cost and 75% LTV the site already states, which is the check that
the deals are being read correctly.

**Format rules.** First line carries the post — LinkedIn truncates around 200
characters and the rest is behind "see more". Short paragraphs, one idea each.
3-5 hashtags, at the end. No emoji walls. No "🚀". These readers are allergic
to marketing voice and fluent in numbers.

## Post 1 — The ratio that actually binds

> Most investors quote me an ARV. Almost nobody quotes me a cost basis.
>
> That's backwards, because on nearly every deal I fund it's loan-to-cost that
> sets the check size — not loan-to-ARV.
>
> Two we funded, same lens:
>
> Fix & flip — $185,000 purchase, $62,000 rehab. Total cost $247,000. Loan
> $208,650. That's 84.5% of cost, but only 61.4% of the $340,000 ARV.
>
> Ground-up — $95,000 lot, $410,000 build. Total cost $505,000. Loan $454,500.
> 90% of cost, 69.9% of the $650,000 finished value.
>
> In both cases the ARV had room. Cost is what governed.
>
> Why it matters: if you're underwriting your own deal off ARV, you'll be
> surprised at the closing table by how much cash you're bringing. Run it off
> total project cost first. The ARV test is the second gate, not the first.
>
> On those two, borrower equity came in at $38,350 and $50,500 respectively.
> Know that number before you go under contract, not after.
>
> #RealEstateInvesting #FixAndFlip #GroundUpConstruction #PrivateLending

*Why it lands: it corrects a habit most borrowers have, with arithmetic they
can check. Nobody argues with division.*

## Post 2 — An 18-month term is a carry decision

> A builder asked me last month why we'd written 18 months on a build he
> expected to finish in 11.
>
> Because the term isn't a prediction of your build time. It's protection
> against your exit.
>
> On a ground-up, you have two clocks running. The construction clock, which
> you partly control — weather, inspectors, the framer who takes another job.
> And the sale clock, which you don't control at all.
>
> Finish in month 11 and list into a slow market, and a 12-month term turns a
> profitable project into a refinance scramble at exactly the moment you have
> the least leverage.
>
> The extra six months costs carry. An extension costs carry plus a fee plus
> whatever the market does to you while you're negotiating.
>
> Price the term for the sale you might get, not the build you're planning.
>
> #GroundUpConstruction #RealEstateDevelopment #ConstructionLoans #Homebuilding

*Why it lands: experienced builders have personally been burned by this. It
reads as someone who has watched it happen rather than someone selling a term.*

## Post 3 — Your draw schedule is a working capital instrument

> The fastest way to stall a rehab has nothing to do with financing. It's a
> draw schedule written by someone who has never paid a contractor.
>
> Your GC wants money at framing. Your draw funds after inspection. That gap —
> usually one to two weeks — comes out of your pocket.
>
> Budget to the dollar and you don't just slow down. Your crew leaves for
> someone else's job, and getting them back costs you more than the gap did.
>
> The fix costs nothing: line your draw stages up against the contractor's
> payment schedule before you sign the scope of work. Not after you're funded.
>
> A $62,000 rehab we funded closed in 11 days and ran clean for exactly that
> reason — the stages and the invoicing were agreed on day one.
>
> Every lender will fund your rehab. Fewer will tell you the schedule is a
> cash-flow decision and not paperwork.
>
> #FixAndFlip #RealEstateInvesting #Rehab #PrivateLending

## Post 4 — Sell, or keep it

> Project's finished and you're looking at a number. The instinct is to sell.
> Sometimes that's wrong.
>
> The question isn't what the house is worth. It's what the rent covers.
>
> One we refinanced: $310,000 property, $2,650 a month. Loan $232,500 at 75%
> LTV. No tax returns — the property's income qualified it.
>
> Sell, and you take the spread once, pay short-term capital gains on it, and
> start the next deal from zero.
>
> Keep it on a DSCR loan, and you pull most of your capital out, keep the
> asset, and the tenant services the debt.
>
> Not an argument for holding everything. It's an argument for running both
> numbers before you call the agent — because the exit you default to is
> usually the one you've modelled, and most flippers have only ever modelled
> one.
>
> #DSCR #BRRRR #RealEstateInvesting #RentalProperty
