The Fed Held Rates in October -- Here's Where Investor Financing Stands Entering November

November 1, 2026 5 min read

After raising rates a quarter point in September -- its first hike since July 2023 -- the Federal Reserve held the federal funds rate steady at 3.75%-4.00% on October 28, with the FOMC approving its policy statement by a 10-2 vote. Mortgage rates didn't wait around for the decision: Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed average had already eased to roughly 7.32% in its October 22 survey, down from a six-week high of 7.40%. Two different signals, one week apart. Here's what each one actually means for a deal you're underwriting right now.

What the Fed Actually Did on October 28

The Fed's own statement, published the same day, confirms the hold at 3.75%-4.00% -- not a cut, and not a pause before more hikes were taken off the table. That distinction matters, because this is a hiking cycle the Fed entered in September, not an easing cycle it's winding down from. The central bank's next scheduled decision is December 9, and as of late October, futures markets were still pricing meaningful odds of another quarter-point increase at that meeting. If you've been waiting for the Fed to start cutting before you refinance or sell, the data right now doesn't support that assumption.

Why a Fed Hold Doesn't Move Your Rate the Way You'd Expect

The federal funds rate and your 30-year mortgage rate aren't the same lever. Mortgage pricing tracks the 10-year Treasury yield and investor expectations about inflation and Fed policy more directly than it tracks the fed funds rate itself. That's why the 30-year average eased slightly in the week before the Fed's meeting, not because of it -- bond markets were already positioning for a hold. It's also why a year ago, with the Fed in a very different posture, the 30-year average sat at 6.19%, more than a full point below where it closed out October 2026. Rates don't move because the news says they should; they move on what the bond market has already priced in.

The headline number to remember: Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed average ran from 7.03% on October 1 to 7.40% by October 15, then eased to roughly 7.32% by October 22 -- still up more than a full point from 6.19% a year earlier.

Where That Leaves Different Types of Investor Financing

Conventional 30-year (Freddie Mac PMMS)~7.32% as of Oct. 22, 2026 -- up from 6.19% a year ago
DSCR rental financingHas held in a roughly 6.0%-8.75% range through 2026, generally steadier than hard money since it's long-term and fully amortizing
Fix & flip / hard moneyPriced on the deal (LTC, LTV, ARV, points) more than on daily rate headlines -- see our breakdown of what a hard money loan actually costs

The takeaway isn't that one product is "cheaper" than another in isolation -- it's that conventional mortgage pricing is the most rate-headline-sensitive of the three, which is exactly why it moves the buyer pool you're selling into, even when it barely moves your own financing cost on a bridge loan.

What "Hold, Not Cut" Means for Deals in Underwriting Right Now

  • Underwrite your exit refinance at today's rate, not a rate you're hoping the Fed delivers by your maturity date
  • If your fix and flip loan has an extension option, know the cost and trigger now -- don't assume you'll beat the clock with a rate-driven rush of buyers
  • Re-run your DSCR at current pricing before you make an offer; a ratio that worked in August may not clear today's qualifying threshold
  • If you're buying down points to hit a rate target, confirm the breakeven math still works at a 5-7 month typical flip hold, not a 30-year amortization schedule
  • Watch the December 9 FOMC meeting -- another hike would widen, not close, the gap between where rates sit now and where many buyers budgeted a year ago

The Bottom Line

A Fed hold reads as good news in a headline, but it isn't the same thing as rate relief. Mortgage pricing is still elevated well above where it sat a year ago, the Fed's own policy stance remains tilted toward fighting inflation rather than supporting growth, and the next meeting could move the needle further in either direction. Build your deal to work at today's numbers, and treat any further easing as upside -- not as the plan.
