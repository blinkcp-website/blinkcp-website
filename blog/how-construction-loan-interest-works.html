How Construction Loan Interest Actually Works: Why You Only Pay on What's Drawn

October 15, 2026 5 min read

First-time builders often budget construction loan interest the same way they'd budget a mortgage payment — as a fixed monthly number based on the full loan amount. That's not how it works, and the gap between that assumption and reality is one of the more common budgeting mistakes on a ground-up build. Here's how construction loan interest is actually calculated, and why the number grows every month of the build instead of staying flat.

Interest Accrues Only on What's Been Drawn

A ground-up construction loan isn't disbursed in one lump sum — it's released in draws as work is completed and inspected. The lender only charges interest on the portion of the loan that's actually been disbursed, not on the full approved loan amount sitting undrawn. If your loan is approved for $400,000 but only $120,000 has been drawn so far to cover the foundation and framing, you're paying interest on $120,000, not $400,000.

Why the Monthly Payment Grows As the Build Progresses

Because each draw adds to your outstanding balance, your interest-only payment increases every time a draw is funded — small early in the project, largest right before completion when nearly the full loan is outstanding. A builder who assumes a flat monthly interest cost based on the total loan amount will overbudget early in the project and, more dangerously, underbudget toward the end.

Example draw stageIllustrative cumulative drawn balance on a $400,000 construction loan
Site work & foundation$60,000 drawn — interest accrues on $60,000
Framing & dry-in$180,000 drawn — interest accrues on $180,000
Mechanicals & drywall$290,000 drawn — interest accrues on $290,000
Finishes & final$400,000 drawn — interest accrues on the full $400,000

This is illustrative, not a quote — actual draw sizing and timing depend on your specific budget and schedule. But the shape of the curve holds across virtually every build: interest cost compounds as the project nears completion, which is exactly when delays are most expensive.

Why This Catches Builders Off Guard

Two things make this mechanic easy to underestimate. First, the rising monthly payment can strain cash flow late in a project, right when change orders and finish-stage costs also tend to cluster. Second, any delay — permitting holdups, inspection scheduling, material lead times, a subcontractor falling behind — extends the period during which a large outstanding balance is accruing interest, directly increasing total project cost beyond what the original timeline assumed.

Interest Reserves vs. Paying Out of Pocket

Many construction loans let you fund an interest reserve — an amount, often built into the loan itself, set aside specifically to cover these monthly interest payments as they come due, rather than pulling from your own operating cash during the build. This doesn't change how much interest accrues, but it does change where the cash comes from, which matters if your capital is already fully allocated to the build budget. Ask your lender whether an interest reserve is available and how it's sized before you assume you'll need to cover payments out of pocket.

What Happens at Completion

Once the certificate of occupancy is issued and the build is complete, the construction loan either converts to permanent financing (on a one-time close structure) or is paid off via a refinance into a long-term loan — commonly a DSCR loan if the exit is to hold the property as a rental. At that point, the interest-only construction period ends and a final reconciliation confirms the total interest accrued against what was budgeted.

How to Budget for It Accurately

  • Model interest cost against your actual projected draw schedule, not a flat average of the total loan amount
  • Build in a timeline cushion — even a modest schedule slip meaningfully increases total interest once most of the loan is drawn
  • Ask your lender whether an interest reserve is available and how large a reserve the loan supports
  • Reconcile projected vs. actual interest cost at each draw, not just at the end of the project
  • Factor total accrued interest into your as-built cost basis before deciding whether to sell or refinance into a rental

The Bottom Line

Construction loan interest is calculated on your outstanding drawn balance, not your full approved loan amount — which means the real cost is smallest at groundbreaking and largest right before completion. Budgeting against the actual draw curve, rather than a flat monthly estimate, is the difference between a project that lands on budget and one that runs short in the final stretch.
