Single-Family Starts and Permits Fell in September: What Cooling Construction Activity Means for Land and Spec Deals

November 1, 2026 5 min read

The Census Bureau and HUD's September new residential construction report, released October 20, shows single-family housing starts fell 4.6% from August to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of roughly 859,000 units. Single-family permits -- new construction authorized but not yet started -- hit their lowest rate since March 2023. Total starts (single-family plus multifamily) came in at 1,221,000, down 7.4% from a year earlier. None of this is a surprise if you've been watching builder sentiment, but it's worth understanding exactly what's slowing down and what it means for your next build.

This Follows Builder Confidence at a 14-Year Low

The starts and permits data lines up with what we covered in October's NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index, which fell to 29 -- the lowest reading since June 2012 -- as a record share of builders cut prices to move inventory. Builders don't pull permits for homes they don't believe they can sell at a profitable price, so permits leading starts lower is the expected sequence when confidence craters: fewer permits now means fewer starts in the months ahead, which in turn means a thinner pipeline of finished new construction competing with your spec build next year.

What It Means for Land Acquisition and Trade Availability

A pullback in large builders' spec activity tends to create two openings for smaller, disciplined investors. First, less competition for buildable lots from production builders who were absorbing inventory at volume -- land sellers facing fewer bids are often more willing to negotiate on price or terms. Second, subcontractors and trades who were booked out by larger developments often have more open capacity, which can shorten the wait between your permit approval and your first subcontractor mobilizing on site.

What It Means for Your Draw Schedule and Underwriting

Lenders read the same housing-starts data you do, and a weaker permit environment typically means more scrutiny on your absorption assumptions -- how realistic your projected sale timeline is once the home is complete. Build extra time into your pro forma for marketing and sale, keep your contingency reserve fully funded rather than treating it as a cushion you'll skip drawing on, and make sure your comps reflect current, not peak-era, absorption speeds in your specific submarket.

  • Get updated comps on absorption speed (not just sale price) for finished new construction in your target submarket
  • Re-confirm trade availability and pricing before you lock your draw schedule -- capacity may have loosened since your last build
  • Negotiate land price and terms with the knowledge that large builders are pulling back, not competing as aggressively for lots right now
  • Keep contingency reserves intact rather than assuming a soft market means costs will come in under budget

Why Small, Custom Builders Are Still Finding Work

As we noted when builder confidence hit its 14-year low, the pullback has been sharpest among large production and spec builders, while small-scale and custom builders have reported comparatively steadier conditions. If you're building one or two homes at a time with a specific buyer profile in mind rather than competing on volume against national builders, a slower macro print doesn't necessarily mean a slower pipeline for your own projects.

The Bottom Line

September's starts and permits data confirms what builder sentiment surveys have been signaling for months: large-scale production building is pulling back. For investors building at a smaller scale, that pullback can mean better land pricing, more available trade capacity, and less competition from spec inventory -- provided your underwriting reflects the slower absorption timelines the same data is pointing to.
