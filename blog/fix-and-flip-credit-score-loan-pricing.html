How Credit Score Actually Affects Your Fix and Flip Loan Pricing

October 25, 2026 5 min read

"Hard money doesn't care about credit" is a half-truth that trips up a lot of first-time flippers. It's true that fix and flip lenders underwrite the deal first -- the property, the rehab budget, the ARV -- rather than running you through the same income-and-DIT gauntlet a conventional mortgage requires. But credit score doesn't disappear from the equation. It shows up quietly, in your rate, your points, and sometimes your maximum leverage. Here's where it actually matters.

Why Hard Money Lenders Still Pull Credit

Fix and flip loans are asset-based, meaning the property itself is the primary collateral and the deal's numbers drive the underwriting decision far more than they would on a conventional mortgage. That doesn't mean credit is ignored -- it's used as a secondary risk signal, a way for a lender to gauge how you've historically managed debt, rather than a gatekeeping threshold the way it functions at a bank. A thin credit file or a recent serious delinquency can still raise questions, even on a deal where the real estate itself looks strong.

Where Credit Score Shows Up in Pricing

Credit typically affects three things on a fix and flip loan: the interest rate, the origination points charged at closing, and in some cases the maximum leverage (LTC or LTV) a lender will offer.

The pattern is directionally consistent across most hard money lenders, even though exact pricing grids vary: borrowers with stronger credit profiles tend to see better rate and point combinations, and sometimes access to higher leverage, than borrowers with weaker credit on an otherwise identical deal. The gap is typically much narrower than you'd see on a conventional mortgage, where credit score tiers can swing pricing dramatically -- but it's rarely zero.

Credit Score vs. Track Record: Which Matters More

First-time flipper, strong creditCredit does more of the work, since there's no completed-project history to lean on yet
Experienced flipper, average creditA documented track record of completed, profitable projects often carries more underwriting weight than a marginal credit score
Experienced flipper, strong creditUsually the best combination for pricing and leverage, since both risk signals point the same direction

This is one of the more encouraging realities for investors who've been in the business a while: a completed track record of successful projects can offset a credit score that wouldn't clear a conventional lender's bar, in a way that simply isn't possible on an owner-occupied mortgage.

What If Your Credit Took a Hit on a Previous Deal

A past bankruptcy, foreclosure, or short sale -- even one tied to real estate investing rather than personal finances -- is something hard money lenders will ask about directly, and seasoning requirements (how much time has passed since the event) vary by lender. Be upfront about it early in the conversation rather than letting it surface during underwriting; a lender who understands the full picture up front can often still structure a deal, where one who finds it mid-process may not be able to move as quickly.

How to Strengthen Your Profile Before You Apply

  • Pull your own credit report before you apply so you know what a lender will see, and dispute any inaccuracies well ahead of time
  • Pay down revolving balances where you can -- credit utilization moves faster than most other factors and can lift a score meaningfully in a short window
  • Avoid opening new credit lines or financing a vehicle in the months before you apply for a fix and flip loan
  • Document your project history in a simple track record -- address, purchase price, rehab budget, sale price, and timeline -- even if your lender doesn't ask for it upfront
  • Be transparent about any past credit events early in the conversation rather than hoping they won't come up

The Bottom Line

Credit score isn't the gatekeeper on a fix and flip loan that it is on a conventional mortgage, but it's not irrelevant either -- it's one input among several, alongside the deal itself and your track record, that shapes the rate and leverage you're offered. Know where your credit stands before you apply, and lean on a documented project history if your score isn't where you'd like it to be.
