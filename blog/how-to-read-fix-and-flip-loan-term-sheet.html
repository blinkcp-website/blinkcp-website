How to Read a Fix and Flip Loan Term Sheet Before You Sign

November 1, 2026 6 min read

A term sheet is usually the first real document you get after a lender likes your deal, and it's tempting to skim it, sign it, and move on to the rehab plan. That's a mistake. A term sheet sets the frame for every number you'll see at closing, and the lines that look like boilerplate are often the ones that change your actual cost of capital the most. Here's how to read one line by line.

What a Term Sheet Is -- and Isn't

A term sheet is a preliminary, non-binding outline of the loan a lender is prepared to offer based on what you've told them and an early look at the property. It is not a commitment, and most of its terms are explicitly conditioned on things that haven't happened yet -- an appraisal, a title search, a final review of your rehab budget. Treat it as a strong indication of intent, not a locked-in promise, and read every "subject to" clause as a place where the final numbers could still move.

The Loan Amount Line: LTC, LTV, and ARV

The single most important line on the sheet is how your loan amount is calculated. Lenders typically cap it at the lowest of a few ratios -- loan-to-cost, loan-to-value on the as-is purchase, and a percentage of after-repair value. We cover the mechanics in detail in our breakdown of LTC vs. LTV vs. ARV, but at the term sheet stage, confirm which ratio is actually binding for your deal -- it's usually not the most generous one, and it's the one that determines how much cash you need to bring to closing.

Ask directly: "Which ratio -- LTC, LTV, or ARV -- is the one actually capping my loan amount on this deal?" If the lender can't answer immediately, the number on the term sheet may be more optimistic than what underwriting ultimately approves.

Rate, Points, and the Fees That Aren't on the First Page

Interest rate and origination points are usually front and center, but they're not the whole cost of the loan. Look for a line itemizing underwriting fees, processing fees, a draw inspection fee per draw, and any junior lien or broker fee if one applies. A lower headline rate paired with a longer list of itemized fees can cost more over a typical 6- to 12-month hold than a slightly higher rate with fewer add-ons -- run the all-in cost, not just the rate, against your project timeline.

Term Length, Extensions, and Prepayment

Most fix and flip loans run 6 to 18 months. Confirm the stated term, then look specifically for the extension terms: is an extension automatic for a fee, or does it require a new underwriting review? What's the extension fee, and can you request it before you're already past maturity? On the other end, check for a prepayment penalty or minimum interest guarantee -- some loans charge a minimum number of months of interest even if you sell and pay off early, which changes the math if your rehab finishes ahead of schedule.

Conditions That Can Still Change Your Terms

The term sheet's numbers are typically contingent on a satisfactory appraisal confirming your ARV, a clean title report, proof of builder's risk insurance, and a lender review of your contractor and draw schedule. If any of these come back different than expected -- a lower appraised ARV, a title issue, an unlicensed contractor -- the lender can and often will revise the final loan amount or terms before closing. Ask which conditions are still outstanding and roughly how long each typically takes, so you're not caught off guard by a shifted closing date.

A Pre-Sign Checklist

  • Confirm which ratio (LTC, LTV, or ARV) actually caps your loan amount
  • Add up all fees -- not just points -- as a dollar total against your expected hold period
  • Check the extension process, fee, and whether it requires new underwriting
  • Check for a prepayment penalty or minimum interest requirement if you expect to finish early
  • Ask what conditions (appraisal, title, insurance, contractor approval) are still outstanding
  • Confirm how long the term sheet itself is valid before the rate or terms can change

The Bottom Line

A term sheet is a starting point for negotiation and diligence, not a document to rubber-stamp. The deals that go smoothly are usually the ones where the investor asked these questions in week one -- not at the closing table, when there's far less room to push back.
