Lumber and Material Costs in 2026: What Tariffs Mean for Your Construction Budget

October 12, 2026 6 min read

If you've been assuming tariffs on Canadian lumber mean your framing package is about to get more expensive, the data tells a stranger story. Builder material costs are up overall, but the single category everyone expected to spike — softwood lumber — has actually been falling. Here's what's really moving your construction budget, and why a blanket assumption about "material costs" can lead you to budget for the wrong risk.

The Headline Number: Material Prices Up 3.5% in a Year

The National Association of Home Builders reports that building material prices rose 3.5% between 2024 and 2025 — the largest annual increase in nearly three years. That's a meaningful move for a construction budget built on thin margins, and it's the number worth building contingency around. But it's an average across dozens of material categories, and the categories aren't moving together.

The Lumber Paradox: Tariffs Up 45%, Prices Down

In 2025, the Commerce Department raised antidumping and countervailing duties on Canadian softwood lumber from 14.5% to 35%, and added a 10% Section 232 tariff on timber and lumber imports — pushing the combined duty on Canadian softwood lumber to roughly 45%. Despite that, softwood lumber was one of the few material categories to see prices decline over the same period.

That split isn't a data error — it's supply and demand working against the tariff. Builders pulled back on starts through 2025, and with fewer homes being built, lumber demand fell faster than tariffs could push supply costs up. Several North American mills were reportedly running at a loss and curtailing production, but the drop in builder demand still outweighed the tighter supply.

Why This Matters for Your Draw Budget

If you budgeted a ground-up build assuming "materials are up" across the board, you likely overbudgeted framing and underbudgeted everything else — windows, appliances, electrical components, HVAC equipment, and other manufactured goods are the categories actually driving that 3.5% average higher. The practical lesson: price your framing package close to the quote you get today, but build a wider contingency into categories exposed to tariffs on non-lumber imports, which haven't seen the same demand offset.

How to Budget Around Volatile Material Costs

  • Get a current, written material quote for your framing package rather than assuming last year's lumber pricing narrative still applies
  • Build a larger contingency line into manufactured components — appliances, windows, HVAC equipment, electrical gear — where tariff exposure hasn't been offset by falling demand
  • Ask your builder which materials in your specific scope are imported versus domestically sourced; tariff exposure varies by product, not just by category
  • Revisit your draw-stage budget each time you lock a new supplier quote, rather than carrying the original estimate through the whole build
  • Favor a lender with fast draw turnaround — locking in a supplier price quickly matters more when category-level costs are this uneven

The Bottom Line

"Material costs are up" is true on average and misleading in the specifics. Lumber — the category builders worry about most — has actually offered some relief, while tariff-exposed manufactured goods are doing more of the work pushing the overall index higher. Budget line by line against current quotes, not against last year's headline.
