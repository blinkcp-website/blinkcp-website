How to Build a Rehab Scope of Work and Budget Before You Ever Call a Lender

October 11, 2026 6 min read

A lot of first-time flippers build their scope of work the same way they'd plan a weekend project — a rough mental list, a few contractor conversations, a number that feels about right. Then they apply for financing and discover the lender wants a line-item budget, and that the vague version they had in their head doesn't produce the loan amount they expected. Your scope of work isn't paperwork you fill out for the lender after the fact — it's the document your entire deal gets underwritten against, and building it properly before you apply changes what you can borrow.

Why Your Scope of Work Comes Before Your Application, Not After

Lenders don't fund "renovate the house" — they fund a specific, costed list of work, because that list is what the draw schedule, the after-repair value estimate, and your loan amount are all built around. A vague budget doesn't just slow down underwriting; it tends to get trimmed to whatever the lender's reviewer can verify, which can mean a lower approved rehab budget than you actually need. Walking in with a detailed scope puts you in control of that number instead of the underwriter.

What Goes Into a Line-Item Scope of Work

Structural & systemsRoof, foundation, electrical panel/wiring, plumbing, HVAC — the items an inspection or appraisal will flag if skipped
ExteriorSiding, windows, landscaping, driveway/walkways, paint
Interior finishesFlooring, drywall/paint, kitchen, bathrooms, fixtures, trim
Permits & soft costsPermit fees, architect/engineer costs where required, dumpster/disposal, utilities during rehab
ContingencyA reserve for the unknowns a pre-rehab walkthrough can't catch

Each line should carry a cost estimate from an actual contractor bid or a documented per-unit cost, not a round number pulled from memory. Lenders — and your own margin — are better served by five conservative, bid-backed numbers than one optimistic lump sum.

Building Your Contingency Reserve

A contingency reserve of 10-15% of the total rehab budget is standard practice on most fix and flip projects, and higher on older homes or any property you haven't been able to fully inspect before closing (occupied properties, homes with unknown mechanicals, or anything with visible deferred maintenance).

Margins have been tight enough recently that a contingency reserve isn't optional cushion — it's what keeps a project profitable when something unexpected shows up. ATTOM's Q1 2026 flipping data put the typical gross flip profit at $66,000 on a 25.4% margin; an unbudgeted $8,000 repair discovered mid-rehab is a meaningfully larger bite out of that number than it would have been in a higher-margin market.

How Lenders Use Your Scope of Work to Set Your Loan Amount

Your rehab budget feeds directly into the loan-to-cost and after-repair-value calculations that determine your loan amount — the lender is underwriting the stronger (tighter) of the two, not just the more generous one. A realistic, well-documented budget supports a stronger ARV case because it shows the lender exactly what work produces that value. An underdocumented budget, even a generous one, is harder for an underwriter to tie to a specific after-repair value — which is often where approved loan amounts come in lower than expected.

Common Scope of Work Mistakes That Shrink Your Loan Amount

  • Submitting a single lump-sum rehab number instead of a line-item breakdown by trade
  • Using verbal contractor estimates instead of written bids the lender can review
  • Leaving out permit costs, disposal fees, and soft costs that add up to real money
  • Skipping a contingency line entirely, then requesting a budget increase mid-project
  • Scoping finishes above what comparable sales in the area actually support

The Bottom Line

A detailed, bid-backed scope of work isn't busywork before you apply — it's the document that determines how much you can borrow and how smoothly your draws release once work starts. Build it like you expect it to be reviewed line by line, because it will be.
