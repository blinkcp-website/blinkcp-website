Spec Building vs. Build-to-Suit: How Financing Differs for Ground-Up Construction

October 17, 2026 5 min read

Two builders can pour the same foundation on the same street and carry very different risk. One is building on the bet that a buyer will show up once the home is finished. The other already has a signed buyer and a defined scope before the first shovel goes in the ground. Lenders price that difference, and it shows up in leverage, reserves, and how closely your deal gets underwritten. Here's how spec and build-to-suit financing actually diverge.

Spec Building: Betting on a Buyer

A speculative, or "spec," build means you're constructing a home without a confirmed buyer lined up -- you're building to market demand and will sell once it's complete, the same way a flip works but with new construction instead of renovation. The upside is flexibility: you control the design and finish level to maximize appeal to whoever's buying in that market when you finish. The downside is that you're carrying full market risk through construction and through however long it takes to sell afterward.

Build-to-Suit: Building for a Known Buyer

Build-to-suit means the end buyer -- or at least the end use -- is locked in before construction starts, whether that's a presale contract, a long-term hold you intend to rent out yourself, or a custom home contracted directly with an owner-occupant client. The defining feature isn't who's paying; it's that the exit is substantially de-risked before you break ground.

How Lenders Treat Each Differently

Exit riskSpec: unconfirmed -- underwritten against market comps and absorption data  |  Build-to-suit: substantially confirmed by a presale contract or defined end use
Typical leverageSpec: generally more conservative loan-to-cost given unproven exit  |  Build-to-suit: often supports higher leverage since the exit is contracted
Underwriting focusSpec: local absorption rates, comparable new-construction sales, builder's track record in that specific market  |  Build-to-suit: the end buyer's contract, deposit, and ability to close
Reserve requirementsSpec: typically higher carrying-cost reserves to cover an unsold, unfinanced hold period  |  Build-to-suit: lighter reserve need since a close date is more predictable
Best fit forSpec: experienced builders in markets with strong, documented absorption  |  Build-to-suit: builders with a presale, a committed rental exit, or a direct custom-home client

Where the Risk Actually Shows Up

The real exposure on a spec build isn't the construction phase itself -- it's the gap after a certificate of occupancy and before a closed sale. A spec home that finishes into a slower market can sit for months, during which you're still carrying interest, taxes, insurance, and utilities with no revenue offsetting any of it. Build-to-suit structures largely eliminate that gap, which is exactly why lenders are willing to extend more leverage against them: the risk they're underwriting ends when the house is done, not whenever a buyer eventually shows up.

Converting a Spec Build Into a Build-to-Suit Mid-Project

It's increasingly common for builders to start a project spec and land a buyer partway through construction -- either a retail purchaser or an investor planning to hold it as a rental. If that happens, tell your lender immediately. A presale contract that lands mid-build can sometimes unlock better terms on the remaining draws or set up a smoother transition into permanent financing, but only if your lender knows about it before the loan matures, not after.

  • If you're building spec, confirm your local absorption data and recent new-construction comps before you lock your budget, not just your as-built comps
  • Size your carrying-cost reserve for a realistic sell period in your market, not the best-case scenario
  • If you have any indication of a buyer -- even informal interest -- get it documented, since a soft commitment can still help your underwriting story
  • For build-to-suit, confirm your buyer's deposit and financing contingency are solid before you rely on that contract in your own underwriting
  • Ask your lender directly how their leverage and reserve requirements differ between the two structures before you choose one

The Bottom Line

Spec and build-to-suit aren't just two labels for the same loan -- they're two different risk profiles that lenders underwrite, leverage, and reserve for differently. Know which one your project actually is before you apply, and if your exit changes mid-build, tell your lender right away rather than letting the loan structure fall out of sync with the deal.
