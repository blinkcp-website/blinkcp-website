How to Calculate ARV: A Step-by-Step Comps Method Before You Make an Offer

October 17, 2026 6 min read

After-repair value drives nearly every number in a flip: your maximum loan amount, your rehab budget ceiling, and your exit price all trace back to it. Get ARV wrong by even 5-10%, and you can underwrite a deal that looks profitable on paper but loses money in practice. Lenders and appraisers don't guess at ARV -- they build it from comparable sales, adjusted line by line. Here's the same method, so you can sanity-check a deal before you're under contract, not after the appraisal comes back lower than you hoped.

What ARV Actually Is

ARV is an estimate of what a property will sell for once renovation is complete, based on what similar, already-renovated properties have actually sold for nearby. It's not what you think the house could be worth, and it's not a Zillow Zestimate -- it's a value supported by real closed transactions that a lender's appraiser can independently verify.

Step 1: Pull the Right Comps

Start with closed sales, not active listings -- a listing price is what a seller hopes to get, not what a buyer actually paid. Good comps generally meet these criteria:

  • Sold within the last 3-6 months, ideally the last 90 days in a fast-moving market
  • Within a half-mile to one mile of the subject property, closer in dense urban areas
  • Similar square footage, typically within 10-15%
  • Same bedroom and bathroom count, or close to it
  • Similar lot size, age, and construction style
  • Comparable renovation quality -- a comp should be finished to a similar standard as what you plan to deliver, not a builder-grade flip if you're installing high-end finishes, or vice versa

Pull at least three to five comps if you can find them. Fewer than that, and a single outlier sale can skew your estimate significantly.

Step 2: Adjust for Differences

Comps are rarely identical to your subject property, so each one needs line-item adjustments -- add value for features the comp lacks that your finished property will have, and subtract for features the comp has that yours won't.

Square footageAdjust using a local per-square-foot value for the size difference, not a flat guess
Garage or extra bedroomAdd a market-supported dollar amount if the comp lacks a feature your property will have
Lot size or viewAdjust up or down based on how much buyers in that specific market actually pay for it
Condition at saleIf a comp sold partially renovated, adjust up to reflect a fully finished product
Time since saleIn a market with meaningful month-over-month appreciation or depreciation, adjust older comps for the trend

Step 3: Reconcile to a Single Number

Once each comp is adjusted, you'll have a range rather than one exact figure -- say, three comps that adjust out to $318,000, $326,000, and $334,000. Resist the temptation to anchor to the highest number. Weight your estimate toward the comps that required the fewest and smallest adjustments, since those are the most reliable indicators. A conservative reconciliation in this example would land closer to $320,000-$325,000, not $334,000.

Common Mistakes That Inflate ARV

  • Using comps from outside your actual micro-market because "nearby" comps were scarce -- a different school zone or a busier street can move value more than distance alone suggests
  • Comparing against other investor flips without confirming they actually sold at list price, rather than after a price cut
  • Ignoring days on market -- a comp that sat for 120 days and sold below ask tells you something different than one that sold in a week over ask
  • Using pending sales instead of closed ones, since a pending deal can still fall through or close at a different price
  • Letting the listing agent's opinion of value substitute for your own comp pull -- agents are sometimes motivated to support a higher number to win the listing

Why This Matters for Your Financing

Most fix and flip lenders cap loan proceeds at roughly 65%-75% of ARV, whichever ratio is tightest against your loan-to-cost cap. An inflated ARV doesn't just risk a disappointing sale -- it risks a loan amount that gets revised down after the lender's own appraisal comes in below your number, leaving you short of cash to close or finish rehab. Running a disciplined comps-based ARV before you make an offer is one of the few parts of underwriting entirely within your control.

The Bottom Line

ARV isn't a feeling about what a renovated house should be worth -- it's a number built from real, adjusted comparable sales, the same way an appraiser will build it. Run the comps yourself before you're under contract, reconcile conservatively, and you'll walk into your lender's appraisal with a number that's likely to hold up rather than one that surprises you.
