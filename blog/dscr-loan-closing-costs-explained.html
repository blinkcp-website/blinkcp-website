DSCR Loan Closing Costs Explained: What You'll Actually Pay at the Table

October 10, 2026 5 min read

Most investors run their DSCR math down to the decimal and then get surprised at the closing table by a number they never underwrote: the closing cost total. A DSCR loan's rate and leverage get all the attention, but the cash you need to bring to close is made up of several separate line items, each with its own logic. Here's what's actually in that number, and where you have room to bring it down.

What's Actually Inside "Closing Costs"

"Closing costs" isn't one fee — it's shorthand for three different buckets: fees the lender charges for originating and underwriting the loan, third-party costs the lender doesn't control but collects for, and prepaid items that fund your escrow and interest for the first stretch of the loan. Lumping them together is why the total feels abstract until you break it apart.

Lender Fees You'll See on Every DSCR Loan

Origination pointsTypically 1-2 points of the loan amount, often adjustable for a rate buy-up or buy-down
Underwriting / processing feeA flat fee covering file review, income/DSCR verification, and conditions management
Appraisal feePaid to an independent appraiser to establish value and, often, market rent
Credit report & flood certificationSmall flat fees, usually under $100 combined

Origination points are the biggest lever here, and they're often negotiable against your rate: more points up front can buy a lower rate, fewer points (or a lender credit) raises the rate but reduces cash due at close. Which direction makes sense depends on how long you expect to hold the loan before refinancing or selling.

Third-Party Costs That Aren't the Lender's

These get collected at closing but go to someone other than your lender: title insurance and escrow/settlement fees, recording fees charged by the county, and in some states a mortgage or transfer tax. A handful of states also require an attorney at closing, which adds a legal fee to the total. These costs vary more by property location than by lender, which is why two otherwise-identical deals in different states can show meaningfully different closing cost totals.

Prepaid Interest, Taxes & Insurance Reserves

Prepaid interest: covers interest from your closing date to the end of that calendar month, since most loans don't start their first full payment cycle until the following month.
Escrow reserves: many DSCR loans impound property taxes and insurance, which means funding a cushion of several months' worth at closing — separate from your actual annual tax and insurance bills.

Of everything in a closing cost statement, escrow reserves surprise investors most often, simply because the dollar amount scales with your property's tax and insurance bill, not with your loan amount. A property with a high tax bill or a coastal insurance premium can carry a meaningfully larger reserve requirement than a comparable property in a lower-cost area.

A Sample Closing Cost Breakdown

On a $300,000 DSCR loan, a representative (illustrative, not quoted) breakdown might look like this:

Origination points (1.5%)$4,500
Underwriting & processing fees$1,200-$1,800
Appraisal$550-$700
Title & escrow/settlement$1,500-$2,500 (varies by state)
Recording & miscellaneous$150-$400
Prepaid interest & reserves$1,500-$3,500 (varies by close date and tax/insurance bill)
  • Ask for a full fee breakdown and loan estimate before you commit to a lender, not after you're locked in
  • Compare points-vs-rate scenarios against how long you actually plan to hold the loan
  • Get your insurance quote early — a cheaper policy lowers both your ongoing payment and your escrow reserve at closing
  • Ask whether your lender allows rolling some costs into the loan amount versus paying cash at close
  • Time your closing date deliberately — closing near the start of a month can reduce prepaid interest due

The Bottom Line

Closing costs on a DSCR loan aren't a mystery once you separate them into what the lender charges, what third parties charge, and what simply prepays expenses you'd owe anyway. Ask for an itemized estimate early, and run the total — not just the rate — into your return math before you decide a deal pencils.
