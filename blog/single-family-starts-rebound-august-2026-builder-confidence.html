Single-Family Starts Rebounded in August — But Permits and Builder Confidence Tell a Different Story

September 18, 2026 5 min read

This month's residential construction data is a genuine mixed bag. Single-family homes actually breaking ground jumped sharply in August, which on its own would read as a sign builders are getting more confident. But permits moved the other way, and builder sentiment itself fell to its lowest level in three years. Here's how to reconcile numbers that are pointing in opposite directions.

The August 2026 Numbers

Census Bureau, August 2026: Single-family starts: 918,000 annualized (up 7.6% from July)  |  Single-family permits: 878,000 annualized (down 1.8% from July)  |  Overall housing starts: 1.275 million (down 2.6% from July)  |  NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index: 32 (down 3 points, tying the lowest reading in three years)

Per the Census Bureau's Monthly New Residential Construction report and NAHB's September Housing Market Index release, single-family starts posted their strongest monthly gain in some time even as the two forward-looking indicators — permits and builder sentiment — both softened. That combination is worth sitting with rather than reading as pure good or bad news.

How to Reconcile a Starts Jump With Falling Confidence

Starts measure construction that's already in motion — often projects permitted and planned months earlier, before the latest run-up in mortgage rates and construction costs. A start reported in August reflects a decision made well before August. Builder confidence and permits, by contrast, are forward-looking: they capture how builders feel about starting new projects right now, with today's financing costs and today's buyer demand. The NAHB's own breakdown makes the disconnect explicit — current sales conditions fell four points to 35 and six-month sales expectations dropped six points to 37, both clearly deteriorating, while the pipeline of already-started projects kept moving.

What's Weighing on Builder Sentiment

  • Mortgage rates climbed through September, directly pressuring the pool of buyers who can qualify for a new build at today's prices
  • Labor shortages and rising material costs were both cited explicitly by NAHB as pressuring builder margins
  • 42% of builders rated current lot availability as poor, and another 38% called it only fair — a majority reporting real constraints on buildable, permit-ready land
  • Traffic of prospective buyers held steady at 23, the one component that didn't fall — suggesting interest hasn't disappeared, even as builders are more cautious about converting it into new starts

What a Falling Permits Number Signals Ahead

Starts (August)Up 7.6% — reflects decisions made before the latest rate and cost pressure
Permits (August)Down 1.8% — reflects builders' appetite for new projects right now
Builder confidence32, tied for a three-year low — builders expect the next six months to be tougher, not easier
What it suggestsFewer new ground-up starts are likely in the pipeline for the months ahead unless rates or costs ease

What This Means If You're Planning a Build

  • A cautious builder market with tight lot supply can mean less competition for buildable lots and subcontractor time if you're ready to move — the builders pulling back are opening space, not closing it
  • Underwrite material costs conservatively; labor and material pressure cited by NAHB isn't limited to production builders and will show up in investor-builder bids too
  • With permits trending down, expect less new-construction competition at resale in 12-18 months in markets where permit activity has clearly slowed — a potential tailwind for an investor-built home's eventual sale
  • Confirm lot availability and entitlement status early; with 80% of builders calling lot supply poor or fair, a shovel-ready lot is a real asset, not a formality

The Bottom Line

August's starts number is encouraging on its face, but it's measuring decisions made before the market's latest headwinds. Builder sentiment and permits — the numbers that actually reflect today's appetite for new projects — both point toward a more cautious stretch ahead. For investor-builders with the financing and underwriting discipline to move while bigger builders hold back, that caution can translate into less competition for land, labor, and buyers alike.
