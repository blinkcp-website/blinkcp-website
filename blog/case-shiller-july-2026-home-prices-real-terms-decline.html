Home Prices Rose Just 1.9% in July — and Fell in Real Terms for the 14th Straight Month

October 15, 2026 5 min read

The S&P Cotality Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index, released September 29, showed home prices up just 1.9% year-over-year in July 2026 — a slight pickup from June's 1.6% gain, but still well below the roughly 3.4% pace of inflation over the same period. That means the index has now posted a real, inflation-adjusted decline for 14 consecutive months. If part of your deal math assumes the market will quietly bail out a thin margin, this is the data that says to stop assuming that.

The Headline Number

Nationally, prices rose 0.3% month-over-month on a seasonally adjusted basis in July. That's modest growth in nominal terms — but once you back out inflation, homeowners and investors holding for appreciation alone have now been losing ground in real purchasing power for well over a year.

The Regional Split

National averages mask a wide spread between metros. Case-Shiller's 20-city data for July shows:

Strongest gainersChicago +6.9%  |  New York +5.8%  |  Cleveland +4.2%
Weakest / decliningSeattle -1.6%  |  Las Vegas -1.3%  |  Denver -1.1%

That pattern lines up with the broader regional rotation underway this year — Midwest and Northeast metros with tighter supply continuing to outperform, while several Sun Belt and mountain-west markets that saw the heaviest pandemic-era construction are now working through more inventory relative to demand.

Why "Real Terms" Should Change How You Underwrite

A flip or a buy-and-hold that only pencils because you're counting on the property to simply be worth more by the time you sell or refinance is underwriting to a trend that hasn't shown up in the national data in over a year. Nominal price growth near 2% isn't zero, but it's not a cushion either, especially once carrying costs, selling costs, and financing costs are netted out.

The practical takeaway: treat appreciation as a bonus if it shows up, not as the variable that makes a deal work. A deal that only clears the bar with 4-5% annual appreciation baked in is a deal built on an assumption the current data doesn't support nationally.

What This Means by Strategy

Fix & Flip: a tight ARV that depends on the market drifting upward during your hold period is a riskier bet than it would have been a few years ago. Underwrite to the comps you have today, not where you hope comps will be in six months.

DSCR Rental: this is the environment where cash-flow-first underwriting earns its keep. A property that qualifies on rent today, independent of appreciation, is a sounder hold than one that only works if prices keep climbing.

Ground-Up Construction: in flat-appreciation markets, the spread between build cost and completed value does the work appreciation used to do. Scrutinize your cost basis and comps even more closely before committing to a lot.

Where the Math Still Works

Markets posting real outperformance — Chicago, New York, and Cleveland among them in this report — aren't guaranteed to keep leading, but they illustrate that national averages understate opportunity in markets where supply stayed disciplined relative to demand. Sourcing deals with an eye on local, not national, price trends matters more in a market where the national number is barely keeping pace with inflation.

The Bottom Line

National home prices are still rising, just not by much — and not as fast as the cost of everything else. Fourteen straight months of real-terms decline is a signal to underwrite deals on their own cash flow and cost basis rather than a rising-tide assumption, while paying closer attention to which specific metros are actually bucking the national trend.
