What Happens When Your Fix and Flip Loan Matures Before You Sell

October 14, 2026 6 min read

ATTOM's Q1 2026 flipping data put the typical flip at 165 days from purchase to resale — up from 160 days the previous quarter and 164 days a year earlier. In a market where buyers are taking longer to transact and homes are sitting longer before selling, timelines creeping past what you planned for isn't a worst-case scenario anymore — it's increasingly the median outcome. If your fix and flip loan has a 9- or 12-month term, here's what actually happens if rehab and resale run longer than that.

Why More Flips Are Running Past Their Loan Term

A fix and flip loan term is sized around a plan: a few months of rehab, then a sale within a reasonable marketing window. Two things have been stretching that plan industry-wide — rehab timelines extended by permitting delays, material lead times, and tighter trade labor, and resale timelines extended by a market where buyers have more leverage and more inventory to choose from. Neither problem means the deal failed. It usually just means the math still works, but not on the original clock.

What "Maturity Date" Actually Means

A fix and flip loan is a bridge loan: short-term, typically interest-only, with the full principal balance due in a single balloon payment on the maturity date. That date isn't a soft deadline — it's a contractual obligation. Missing it without an arrangement in place can trigger a default rate (often several points above your normal rate), late fees, and ultimately the lender's right to begin foreclosure, regardless of how close you are to a closing.

The rule of thumb: start the conversation with your lender 30-60 days before maturity, not the week it hits. Lenders have far more flexibility to work with a borrower who flags the issue early than one who goes quiet and misses the date.

Your Options When the Clock Is Running Out

  • Request an extension — the most common path when the property is listed and a sale is realistically close
  • Refinance into a DSCR rental loan — if the exit shifts from selling to holding, a long-term DSCR loan pays off the bridge loan and converts the property into a rental
  • Sell at a price that moves faster — accepting a lower offer can cost less than a default rate and extension fees combined, depending on how far behind pace the sale already is
  • Pay off the loan from other funds — if available, this avoids extension costs and keeps the property free and clear while you continue marketing it

How Loan Extensions Typically Work

Most hard money lenders will extend a performing loan — one where payments have been current and the borrower is actively marketing the property — rather than force a sale or foreclosure that benefits no one. A typical extension isn't free, though. Expect an extension fee (commonly 0.5-2 points on the loan balance), a short additional term (often 60-90 days rather than a full new loan term), and in some cases a modest rate increase. Lenders will generally want to see the listing, recent showing activity or an accepted offer, and confirmation that the project is otherwise on track before approving one.

PathExtension: keeps the same loan, adds cost, buys time to sell at the right price
PathDSCR refinance: exits the bridge loan entirely, converts to a long-term hold if the rental numbers work
PathPrice reduction / fast sale: avoids extension and default costs but may compress or eliminate the planned margin
PathDo nothing: default rate, late fees, and eventually foreclosure exposure — avoid at all costs

Before You Need One: Preparing for an Extension Request

  • Track your rehab and listing timeline against your loan term from day one, not just when maturity is close
  • Keep your lender updated on project progress proactively, even before you need anything from them
  • Build extension cost into your deal math upfront, so it's not a surprise if the timeline slips
  • Have your DSCR refinance numbers run in advance in case the better move turns out to be holding, not selling
  • Get the property listed before the final 60-90 days of your term, not after

The Bottom Line

A loan maturing before a sale closes isn't a failure — it's a timing mismatch, and lenders deal with it regularly in a market where the median flip now takes well over five months. What turns it into a real problem is silence. Flag it early, know your options going in, and an extension or a DSCR refinance can turn a tight deadline into a manageable one.
